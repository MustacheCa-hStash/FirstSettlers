# Village points of interest and suggested layout

Two new planning illustrations for First Settlers, continuing the ivory-paper, ink and gouache catalogs:

- [02: Suggested village layout](02-village-layout.png).
- [Exact image prompts](GENERATION_PROMPTS.md), generated with the built-in image generation tool.

This is a British Iron Age inspired farming settlement, using a loose later Iron Age reference around 200–50 BCE for this study. It is a game proposal, not a reconstruction of one excavated village or a statement that all British regions shared this architecture. The images do not implement buildings, terrain, facility simulation or NPC behavior.

## Overall direction

Make households, harvests, animals and maintenance visibly sustain the village. A settlement needs repeated ordinary activity more than a separate named building for every profession. A loom, hand quern or small repair bench can belong to a household. Larger or well-connected villages can add specialists without making every small farmstead an industrial centre.

The [Avalon Archaeology roundhouse account](https://swheritage.org.uk/avalon-archaeology/our-story/iron-age-roundhouse/) supports household hearths, pottery, wooden containers, textile equipment and querns; it also explicitly cautions that little furniture evidence survives. [Butser's reconstructed buildings and experiments](https://www.butserancientfarm.co.uk/buildings-and-experiments) supply construction references for timber, wattle/daub and thatch. Furnishing layouts and precise object groupings here remain proposals.

Roundhouses are the historical default for this particular visual direction. Northern/western stone traditions also offer useful references: [Historic Environment Scotland's Gurness account](https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/broch-of-gurness/history-and-stories/) describes houses with hearths, storage, sleeping spaces, yards and sheds. Gurness is a regional comparison, not evidence for transplanting an entire broch settlement into this temperate inland layout.

## POI catalog

Numbers match both illustrations. Building dimensions, quantities, placement and gameplay roles below are proposed design values.

| P01 | Household cluster | Six to eight family homes, mainly 8–10 m roundhouse modules for this study; one larger household may be about 12 m. Hearth, cooking/storage pots, baskets, wooden bowls/buckets, sleeping platform, stools, fuel stack and clothing. | Daily cooking, sleep, repair and social contact. | P04 | Household craft yard | A household work shelter and usable doorway apron. Warp-weighted loom and spindles in a sheltered domestic space, wool, basket materials, hewn bench, riven boards, axe and repair tools. | Textile, basketry and wood repair; one mixed domestic yard is enough for an initial village. |
| P05 | Smithing shelter, specialist option | Modest open-sided shelter, low forging hearth, charcoal, leather bellows, hammer, tongs, small anvil/block, stock and scrap. | Repair and toolmaking. A well is an alternative where the specific site supports one. | A recurring household destination. Animal watering and dirty washing take place separately; do not use a picturesque central fountain as the default. |
| P09 | Shared court and exchange | Open earthen court, low benches, a few baskets and temporary trestle/display boards. A larger household beside it can host meetings or feasts. | Shared work, gathering and occasional exchange, without requiring a civic hall or permanent shop street. |
| P10 | Gate, boundary and tracks | Modest bank, fence/hedge or hurdle boundary, one main timber gate, farm track, wheel ruts and plain carrying/cart equipment. | P12 | Fields, grazing and woodland access | Nearby cereal plots, rough pasture, field boundaries, an ard as a prop, harvest sheaves and a path to managed woodland/coppice. | Show where food, fodder and fuel come from; the village should connect to a productive landscape. |

[Butser's Iron Age structures research](https://www.butser.org.uk/iastructures.html) discusses reconstructed agricultural structures and the interpretation of storage pits. Pits are not a universal replacement for stores: soil, moisture, season and the chosen site matter.

The [Kent Historic Environment Record for the Turing College settlement](https://heritage.kent.gov.uk/Monument/MKE102213/) records textile processing and pottery production, with potential metalworking, in differentiated activity areas. This supports considering craft zones; it does not establish our particular forge, firing method, building plan or specialist distribution.

[English Heritage's prehistoric daily-life overview](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/learn/story-of-england/prehistory/daily-life/) describes agricultural/domestic life and regional settlement variation. The proposed grouping deliberately retains that variation rather than treating one complete village service list as mandatory.

## Suggested layout

Use an irregular oval village envelope of approximately **80 × 100 m** for this first study, accommodating six to eight households plus yards, stores and paths. This is an authoring envelope, not a population-density claim. Crop fields and grazing continue outside it. Leave real working space between roof edges; do not count only wall footprints.

For this illustrated location, north is up, the ground falls generally northwest to southeast, a spring/stream lies along the west side, and the assumed prevailing wind is west to east. These are site assumptions for designing this map, not universal ancient village rules.

- **North/northwest:** higher domestic ground and grain storage. The spring collection point is outside the northwest edge, upstream of other village activity.
- **Centre:** a modest shared court. Homes and a larger household address it through short, irregular paths. Household crafting is beside homes, with weaving sheltered from weather.
- **North-centre/northeast:** raised grain stores and a neighbouring grain-processing yard, reachable from the field track without hauling every harvest through the court.
- **East/southeast:** small smithing area at the edge and pottery firing farther outside the boundary, with bare ground and room from thatch and stored harvest. Place them according to actual slope and wind when authoring terrain.
- **Southwest:** livestock pens with their own outward route to pasture. Separate this route from the protected water approach.
- **South:** main gate and a broad worn entry track. A farm route continues outside the enclosure toward the fields.
- **Southeast, outside:** lower service/refuse area. Keep its runoff away from the western spring and collection approach; dirty water should not be drawn into the clean-water system.

For a gameplay blockout, try a 3–4 m main track, 1.5–2 m minor paths, a roughly 14–18 m shared court and varied clear gaps around thatch roofs. These are provisional navigation/readability values, not archaeological measurements or engineering standards. Recheck after characters, carts, animals and roof overhangs exist at native scale.

The map's wind and water arrows explain this particular design. They do not model climate, drainage or fire spread. Its clean-water separation is a practical planning choice; it is not evidence for a documented municipal sanitation system.

## Optional places and settlement variants

A quiet tree, spring margin or small offering place can supply a local story. The map's tiny optional lore nook is not a claim that every Iron Age village had a standardized sacred grove. The game's attunement stone, wards and woodland magic should be explicitly presented as fantasy additions when used.

A larger household can host feasting, visitors, disputes or a healer character without introducing a medieval tavern, town hall or hospital. Formal religious buildings, burial arrangements, permanent trading areas and stronger fortifications should follow a chosen regional/time reference and settlement importance.

For location variants, add a landing/net-drying area to a waterside settlement, a quarry/stone-working yard to a stone-producing settlement, or an outlying smelting site to a metal-producing one. Do not pack every possible activity into every village. Burial practices are too variable to assume a conventional village churchyard.

## Fit with the game's existing assets

Use the installed sandstone court walls/coping, gateposts, bench, trough, steps, retaining wall, drain, shelf and hearth curb to make workyards and terrain edges legible. The orange stone is an art/material choice; it does not require an Egyptian/desert town. Use timber and wattle for lighter enclosures.

Existing F01–F06 furnishings supply seating, tables, a sleeping platform and a shelf. Existing V01–V30 sheets are *concept references* for work racks, firewood, buckets, yokes, baskets/cargo and exchange furniture; do not imply that all of these are installed or functional. For a stricter period pass, favour pots, baskets and modest wooden containers over a repeated visual vocabulary of iron-bound warehouse barrels and merchant crates.

The suggested roundhouses require a future radial/roundhouse construction kit. With current modular parts, keep the same site relationships and substitute square or gabled timber/sandstone-base homes as an explicitly stated gameplay adaptation; do not relabel them as a typical excavated Iron Age house.

Useful next prop/facility priorities are **hearth with pot support, complete hand quern, warp-weighted loom, raised grain store, covered storage pit, livestock gate/fodder rack, water-carrying set, small forging hearth/bellows and grain-processing props**. Most of the village can work as repeated household activity plus a handful of shared destinations.

## Review and limits

Both images use the built-in image generation tool; [exact prompts](GENERATION_PROMPTS.md) are recorded. Review covers POI labels, roundhouse-led silhouette, modest craft facilities, orange sandstone accents, wind/flow direction and separation of clean water, animals, grain and hot/dirty work. Generated positions and counts are illustrative: written layout relationships govern where the image simplifies them.

The selected POI v2 replaces the first draft's horned anvil with a small rectangular iron block. The original is retained as provenance. The layout shows eight homes, P01–P12 markers and an unnumbered optional lore nook. Water arrows run south along the western stream; the assumed wind arrow runs east. Specialist tools and architectural details remain stylized proposals requiring site-specific reference before exact historical reconstruction.

These are planning references only. No Unity scenes, catalog entries, facility simulation, terrain or saved village state are modified by this work.
