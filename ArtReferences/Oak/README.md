# Oak modeling references

Primary species: white oak (Quercus alba). Northern red oak (Quercus rubra) is included to distinguish two common eastern North American forms. These are species examples, not universal traits of every oak.

## Leaf shape and scale

- White oak: elongated blade, 7–9 rounded lobes without bristle tips; often 5–9 inches (13–23 cm) long. Dull green above and paler beneath. [Iowa State leaf, twig and bark photos](https://naturalresources.extension.iastate.edu/forestry/iowa_trees/trees/white_oak.html).
- Northern red oak: elongated blade with 7–11 pointed, bristle-tipped lobes, roughly 7 inches (18 cm) long in the detailed description. [NC State photo gallery and botanical description](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/quercus-rubra/).
- Sugar maple: broad, palmately veined, usually 5 lobes; 3–6 inches (8–15 cm) long and wide. [NC State](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/acer-saccharum/).
- Red maple: broad, 3–5 principal lobes, coarsely toothed margins; about 2–6 inches (5–15 cm) long. [NC State](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/acer-rubrum/).

Oak leaves are often longer than maple leaves, but their narrow lobed shape does not imply proportionately greater surface area. Size ranges overlap.

## Arrangement and canopy

Oaks have alternate leaves: one leaf per node along the twig. Both maples have opposite leaves, paired at each node. See the white oak gallery's “Leaves with alternate arrangement on the stem” and “Branch” images: [NC State white oak gallery](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/quercus-alba/).

White oak grown in the open develops massive spreading limbs and a broad irregular crown. In dense forest it develops a longer clear trunk and narrower crown. [Alabama Forestry Commission illustrated forest tree guide, white oak entry](https://www.forestry.alabama.gov/Pages/Management/Forms/Forest_Trees_Alabama.pdf).

Modeling interpretation: build foliage on fine twigs as overlapping sprays, with irregular gaps showing the large branch structure. Keep variation in leaf angle and overlap, and avoid arranging oak leaves in maple-like mirrored pairs. For a forest variant, raise and narrow the crown; for an open-grown variant, retain larger lower spreading limbs. These are art directions, not measured foliage-density prescriptions.

## Typical mature size references

Approximate reference ranges, not maxima or a guarantee for a particular site:

| Species | Height | Crown spread |
| --- | --- | --- |
| White oak | 50–75 ft / 15–23 m | 40–70 ft / 12–21 m |
| Northern red oak | 50–75 ft / 15–23 m | Approximately as wide |
| Sugar maple | 60–75 ft / 18–23 m | 40–50 ft / 12–15 m |
| Red maple | Commonly 40–70 ft / 12–21 m | 30–50 ft / 9–15 m |

Sources: [white oak, Iowa State](https://naturalresources.extension.iastate.edu/forestry/iowa_trees/trees/white_oak.html), [northern red oak, NC State](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/quercus-rubra/), [sugar maple, Arbor Day](https://shop.arborday.org/treeguide/340), [red maple, NC State](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/acer-rubrum/).

All can exceed these heights, especially forest-grown trees. For game art, a similar oak/maple height range with a wider oak crown is more defensible than making every oak much taller.
