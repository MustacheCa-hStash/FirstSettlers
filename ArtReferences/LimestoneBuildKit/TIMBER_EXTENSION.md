## Period and design judgement

The reference's developed exposed framing, upper storey, boarded window treatment and complex roof composition read as a later vernacular house. That assessment is an architectural interpretation of the supplied image, not a date attributed to a known building. Preserve its material contrast and useful components while simplifying the silhouette for First Settlers.

Timber posts, wattle covered in daub and reed thatch have a strong basis in the project's existing British Iron Age inspired vocabulary. Butser's [Glastonbury M74 reconstruction account](https://www.butserancientfarm.co.uk/blog/2022/3/8/glastonbury-m74-roundhouse-what-goes-up-must-come-down) describes those materials; its [Iron Age area guide](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5c50c0077c93277e627b7374/t/5de52685e6b0e7013ac813d2/1575298696278/Butser-Ancient-Farm-Iron-Age-Area.pdf) also discusses daub, timber, thatch and clay-based wall colouring. These are experimental reconstructions informed by archaeology, not evidence for the reference house's exact form.

English Heritage's [medieval architecture overview](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/learn/story-of-england/medieval/architecture/) discusses developed timber framing and upper-storey timber construction. The Weald & Downland museum's [roofing guide](https://www.wealddown.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/Roofing-Materials.pdf) documents wood shingles as a historic roof covering, including medieval examples. Accordingly, the exposed panel composition, shingle roof and two-storey assembly here are **optional vernacular game extensions**, not a claim that this was a typical Iron Age house. The sources do not establish an Iron Age date for the proposed shingles.

The earlier-facing option uses a simple timber frame, warm clay/daub infill, fewer exposed diagonals and the existing thatch roof. The optional craft extension introduces neater framed panels, split shingles, boarded accents, fascia and a bracketed canopy. Both use wooden pegs and simple squared openings. Omit the reference's Gothic-looking window, elaborate sign bracket, large jetties and complex cross-roof from this first extension. No glazed window or stone-shingle roof is proposed.

The cream colour is a visual palette, not a requirement that all pale wall infill be limestone. Daub/clay finish and optional limewash should remain distinct material/recipe decisions. Limestone is the masonry shell and bearing base; timber carries the light upper floor and roof. A pale appearance alone does not make plaster a structural stone material.

L25–L28 use local X along the span, Y up and Z=−0.125..+0.125 for the structural wall. End posts and rails are included within the full span/height, with nominal 0.25 m-wide primary frame faces. Do not add external post cells to a 4 m panel. The narrower wall leaves a 0.125 m ledge on either side when centred over a 0.50 m masonry wall.

For framed-wall junctions, the simplest independent-panel version has its end posts inside its span, so adjacent panels make a visibly paired post at the boundary. A future junction resolver can share/replace members, but must not silently delete bearing or extend the bay. Straight, corner, T and 45° joints need an actual fitting pass. L25's frame and infill are one wall assembly; loose L29 decorative battens are not a substitute for its frame.

L28 is constructed with a completely open aperture, following the user's no-shutter instruction. Its solid mesh, placement occupancy and physical colliders preserve the same opening. The shutters in the earlier painted sheet are superseded.

L29's 2 m is its **local board length**, not both its horizontal run and rise. A 2 m strip at 45° spans approximately 1.414 m on each axis before considering face width and end cuts. Use cut-to-fit variants, not intersecting full boards protruding through rails. L29 can decorate L07 or other compatible pale walls, but adds 0.075 m outside their surface and must be anchored and represented accordingly. L30 likewise adds 0.10 m surface projection and is best seated immediately below the floor datum, within the storey face rather than above it.

## Bearing, storeys and roofs

Keep floor top/next wall base at Y=3 m, next eaves at Y=6 m and ridge at Y=8 m for the two-storey example. The upper timber floor occupies Y=2.75..3.00. L36 occupies the same top band only at the receiving masonry/beam seats; shortened/resolved masonry and notched/fitted beam ends must prevent overlapping solid volumes. Centred light upper walls start at Y=3. The picture is not proof that the existing wood floor code already supports this mixed joint.

The baseline two-storey shell is 4 × 8 m between wall centrelines. Its masonry lower exterior is 4.50 × 8.50 m; its centred 0.25 m upper walls are 4.25 × 8.25 m outside. There is **no jetty**. A fascia can visually soften the thickness change; it does not support an overhanging upper wall. Existing timber floors, stairwell variants and interior timber construction should be reused after a stone-aware fitting review. Ground paving remains supported by ground; it is never the suspended upper floor.

L31 and L33 share the 2 m rise/run geometry. Pair roof halves across the 4 m span. The installed L33 retains seven exposed courses, a second starter course, solid uneven shingle ends, rafters and battens. Its 0.22 m eave projection is cosmetic and clips away automatically at an uphill join. The occupied shape is a thin sloped slab plus an eave bearing, leaving the attic clear. The normal-to-slope envelope is about 0.24 m; the roof's visual ridge exceeds the logical 2 m rise. L34 remains a separate unbuilt ridge treatment.

L34 is the wooden-shingle ridge treatment. Thatched roofs keep a thatch-appropriate ridge and eave treatment; do not simply put a shingle ridge board on every existing thatch roof. The canopy slopes **down away from its wall**, so its high ledger can be above an opening while its low front edge clears traversal. A proposed installation sets the low eave at Y=2.50 and rear ledger at Y=3.50 relative to the door's floor; lower brackets must sit beside the aperture and never descend into its clear walking lane. Upper windows and storey belts need clearance from that high attachment. Exact complete canopy bounds include the cover thickness and bracket depth stated in the table.

## Assembly and implementation order

The main sheet 08 assembly intentionally uses a simple two-storey rectangle rather than the reference's compound roof. Limestone supplies the ground shell; framed daub, a small boarded accent, square shutters and a plain belt define the upper storey. Shingles, bargeboard and a braced entry canopy supply the optional later-craft silhouette. A side stair and supported landing must arrive at a real upper doorway, with full character clearance and a suitable future guard. Any small railing shown is a fit-study accessory, not a thirteenth numbered item.

The smaller earlier-facing vignette uses a low masonry base and a timber/daub/thatch superstructure. Its palette and construction materials draw on the cited experimental work; its square footprint remains a game adaptation. Neither vignette is a measured plan, a counted bill of materials or a structurally certified design.

Model the framed wall, boarded wall, square opening, window and half-gable first. Reuse existing wood materials, timber floors and thatch when appropriate. Add shingle roof, matching ridge/verge and canopy as one coordinated later pass. Existing W## parts or S16 should be reused for bearing sections rather than manufactured as duplicate geometry under a second ID. Recipes and production stations remain proposals; this extension introduces no balanced crafting costs, durability bonuses or mandatory new wood resource.

For textures, keep clean render separate from timber in the atlas. Framing, braces and seams belong in the mesh or a deliberately baked complete-panel asset; do not paint a fixed Tudor framework into every plaster UV patch. Roof geometry should establish overlap/silhouette while its material supplies broad shingle faces. Follow the [trim-sheet notes](Textures/README.md) before treating the generated surface study as an import-ready atlas.
